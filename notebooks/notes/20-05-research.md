## What the literature implies for this exact problem

The literature most aligned with your current runner is **probability-based label-quality estimation**, not generic anomaly detection. Confident Learning frames the problem as estimating and ranking likely label errors under a class-conditional noise process using predicted probabilities, then pruning, counting, and ranking suspicious examples. That is a strong conceptual fit for your current setup because you already produce chronological OOF probabilities and you already separate “same-parent suspicious” from “opposite-direction suspicious.” citeturn13academia3 fileciteturn0file0

Time-series-specific noisy-label work matters, but it points to a different implementation layer than the one you are using today. CTW was proposed because directly porting noisy-label methods from vision to time series can break temporal dependency; it expands the distribution of **confident** instances using time-warping and normalizes classwise loss distributions to reduce selection bias. Separately, the temporal label-noise literature argues that when label corruption changes over time, methods that explicitly model a time-dependent noise function outperform static noise assumptions. My inference from those sources, combined with your repo design, is that CTW-style and temporal-noise-function methods are better matched to a **future sequence-native branch** than to the current **merged-feature CatBoost runner**. citeturn5view0turn1academia0 fileciteturn5file0 fileciteturn0file0

Co-teaching and DivideMix are important reference points, but they are not the right first move here. Co-teaching exploits the memorization effect of deep nets by training two networks that exchange small-loss examples; DivideMix goes further by splitting the data into “clean” and “noisy” sets and treating the latter more like semi-supervised unlabeled data. Those approaches can work very well, but they are materially heavier than your present CatBoost line and require much more training-diagnostic discipline. Given that your current structured PyTorch branch is still losing on the real gate, these methods are better treated as **later-stage deep baselines**, not immediate next steps. citeturn0academia2turn0academia1 fileciteturn0file0

The literature also supports your instinct to keep feature-space anomaly detectors in a supporting role. Wu and Keogh’s critique of time-series anomaly detection benchmarks is a good warning that “anomaly detection progress” can be overstated on flawed benchmarks. NCAD and Deep SAD are real anomaly-detection methods with useful ideas, but they are aimed at detecting anomalous observations in a broader sense, whereas your “anomaly leaves” mean **same-parent suspicious labels** within a supervised classification task. That makes unsupervised or semi-supervised outlier scoring a potentially useful **weak signal**, but a dangerous **primary relabeling rule**. Your own brief makes the same point explicitly for KMeans: useful as a class-conditional outlier signal, not safe as the sole relabeling rule. citeturn12academia1turn4academia0turn11academia2 fileciteturn0file1

For the structured MLP branch, the literature is clear on two things. First, calibration matters: Guo et al. show that modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated and that temperature scaling is a simple and effective post-hoc method; deep ensembles improve predictive uncertainty as well. Second, robustness in accuracy does **not** imply reliability: Olmin and Lindsten show that training with noisy labels can preserve some accuracy while still failing on calibration and reliable uncertainty. That maps almost perfectly to your PyTorch pilot, which improved some directional or minority-class behavior while getting much worse on ECE, Brier score, and logloss. citeturn0academia3turn11academia0turn5view2 fileciteturn0file0

The robust-loss literature is useful, but it needs to be applied selectively. Focal Loss down-weights well-classified examples and concentrates learning on hard examples, which is attractive for sparse anomaly leaves. Class-Balanced Loss reweights classes using the “effective number of samples,” which is directly relevant when anomaly leaves are rare. Generalized Cross Entropy provides a principled interpolation between cross-entropy and MAE for noisy labels, while Symmetric Cross Entropy explicitly targets the combination of overfitting and under-learning under noise. Label smoothing can improve calibration and can be competitive under some noisy-label settings, but later work also shows that in higher-noise regimes it can over-smooth and lose its advantage. So for your PyTorch branch, the most sensible order is **temperature scaling first, then mild label smoothing, then imbalance-aware weighting/focal**, and only after that the more aggressive GCE/SCE ablations. citeturn10academia0turn19view1turn19view2turn3academia1turn11academia1turn10academia2turn16academia1turn16academia3

Multi-head models are defensible in principle, because auxiliary direction and regime heads reflect real structure in your target. More generally, multitask learning can improve representation quality when related tasks share useful signal and the losses are balanced properly. But the repo evidence matters more than the theory right now: your current structured prototype still fails because the final business metric is the collapsed 4-class task, not the internal auxiliary heads. So multi-head modeling is justified **only if** a plain 4-class MLP first gets close to the CatBoost baseline and a calibrated fusion strategy improves the actual collapsed 4-class objective. citeturn10academia1 fileciteturn0file0

## Leakage risks, failure modes, and promotion gates

The biggest technical risk is **future leakage through label-quality artifacts**. Your plan is right to forbid one global static anomaly relabel file from being reused across historical walk-forward steps unless each score is filtered by the current step’s training end. If a label-quality score can “see” data that would not yet exist at that historical step, the whole experiment becomes optimistic and unusable. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

A second risk is **substituting the wrong objective**. The business task is not 8-class accuracy. It is the collapsed 4-class task, with special emphasis on reducing `DOWN ↔ UP` errors and preserving usable probabilities. Your own brief already marks rejection cases clearly: if 8-class metrics improve while collapsed 4-class accuracy worsens, if cross-direction error rises, if calibration degrades sharply, or if the anomaly leaves become a generic trash bin, the method should be rejected. That rule is also supported by the broader noisy-label reliability literature: robustness in accuracy alone does not guarantee reliable uncertainty. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5view2

A third risk is **letting opposite-direction disagreement disappear into anomaly leaves**. The repo tests explicitly prevent this, and that safeguard should remain immovable. In this project, same-parent anomalies are meant to isolate hard or suspicious examples **within** a direction/regime family. Confident opposite-direction disagreements are much more dangerous because they can indicate actual direction-label corruption or severe regime mismatch; those belong in review, exclusion, or explicit downweight policies. fileciteturn7file0 fileciteturn0file0

A fourth risk is **overfitting to one market microstructure or session pattern**. The BTCUSDT pilot is useful, but the attached brief is correct that one asset/root pair is not enough for promotion. FX, crypto, equity-index futures, and commodity futures differ enough in session structure, jump behavior, and contextual correlations that any general method should survive that cross-asset panel before you trust it more broadly. fileciteturn0file0

The last major risk is **assuming deep noisy-label methods will rescue a weak base representation**. Co-teaching, DivideMix, CTW, and representation learning methods can all be valuable, but none of them changes the fact that your current deep branch is underperforming on the actual target. Until a simple 4-class structured model becomes competitive and calibrated, adding more sophisticated noise handling is more likely to hide the real problem than solve it. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn0academia2turn0academia1turn5view0turn1academia1
